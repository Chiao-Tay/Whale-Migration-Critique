A data story about Australia's whales

The Humpback Highway

Every winter, whales travel thousands of kilometres along our coast. Where do they go, when can you see them, and how did they come back from the brink?

Chapter 1

Meet the travellers

Two species make up almost every whale report, and each keeps its own calendar.

Between 2016 and 2025, Australians logged 14,019 reports of baleen whales. These are the giant filter-feeders that eat krill and small fish.

Ten species were reported. Two dominate: humpback whales and southern right whales make up nearly 98% of reports.

Throughout this story, humpbacks are blue and southern right whales are orange.

Each species visits at a different time of year. Humpbacks are reported mostly from June to October.

Southern right whales are packed into July and August, which hold 70% of their reports. The few blue whale reports cluster in May and June.

Whales turn up off every state. New South Wales and Victoria recorded the most species, eight each. The Northern Territory recorded just two.

Chapter 2

The great whale highway

Whales follow a coastal highway that fills up in winter and empties in summer.

Whales don't use the ocean evenly. The government has mapped 51 Biologically Important Areas for humpback, southern right, blue and sperm whales.

These are stretches of sea where whales migrate, feed, rest or raise calves. Together they trace a highway around almost the whole coast.

The reports show the highway filling and emptying. In January and February there were just 29 reports nationwide; the whales are away feeding in Antarctic waters.

By July and August, reports climb to 6,770.

Chapter 3

Following the migration wave

The best month to see a humpback depends on where you stand.

Humpbacks swim north in early winter to breed and give birth in warm water. In spring they head back south to Antarctica.

So the busiest month shifts along the coast, like a wave moving north and then back again.

Zoom in on seven east-coast spots and the two directions appear. Sydney has two peaks: whales heading north in June and July, then south in September and October.

At Eden, 52% of reports come in October, as mothers and calves head south. Hervey Bay peaks in August, when whales rest in its sheltered waters.

Chapter 4

Hunted to the brink

The whales we watch today are survivors of an industry that nearly wiped them out.

Whaling stations once operated on both coasts. Their records hold 23,057 humpbacks caught between 1925 and 1963.

2,039humpbacks caught in 1954, the peak year
91caught in 1963, the year humpback hunting was banned

Why the sudden drop? Catches fell from 1,483 in 1961 to 91 in 1963 because there were too few whales left to find.

Over-hunting cut humpbacks to an estimated 3.5–5% of their numbers before whaling. In 1963 the International Whaling Commission banned humpback hunting in the Southern Hemisphere.

Chapter 5

Eyes on the water

Today, ordinary people with phones record most of what we know.

Since 2016, 56% of whale reports came through citizen-science apps such as iNaturalist and Happywhale.

Another 39% came through government wildlife atlases, which also collect many reports from the public.

Scientific surveys and museums, shown in grey, add the rest.

The boom is recent. In 2000, just 3 reports came through citizen-science apps. In 2023 there were 1,876.

This matters when reading the earlier charts. More reports can mean more whales, but also more people looking. That's why they compare shares of reports, not raw totals.
